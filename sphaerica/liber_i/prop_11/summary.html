Analytical Summary: Spherical Angle-Sum Excess¶

Deductive Significance of Proposition I.11

  1. Overturning Euclid Elements I.32: In Euclidean plane geometry, the sum of the three angles of any triangle equals exactly two right angles (\(A + B + C = \pi\)). Menelaus proves the foundational theorem of elliptic/spherical non-Euclidean geometry: on a sphere, the interior angle sum strictly exceeds two right angles (\(A + B + C > \pi\)).

  2. The Antipodal Lune Construction: - Menelaus produces sides \(AB\) and \(A\Gamma\) until they intersect at the antipodal point \(\Delta\) of vertex \(A\). - The two great-circle arcs \(AB\Delta\) and \(A\Gamma\Delta\) form a spherical lune of two full semicircles. - By constructing an angle \(\angle \Delta\Gamma E = \angle A\) at \(\Gamma\), he creates an isosceles spherical triangle \(\triangle \Delta E\Gamma\) whose leg sum with \(BE\) is less than a semicircle (\(BE + E\Gamma = B\Delta < \pi\)). - Applying Proposition I.10 to \(\triangle B\Gamma E\) yields the inequality \(\angle_{\text{ext}} < \angle A + \angle B\), and adding the supplementary angle immediately proves \(A + B + C > \pi\).

  3. Spherical Excess (:term:`excessus-sphaericus`): The difference \(E = A + B + C - \pi > 0\) is the spherical excess, later shown by Thomas Harriot (1603) and Albert Girard (1629) to be strictly proportional to the surface area of the spherical triangle: \(\text{Area}(\triangle ABC) = R^2 E\).
