Menelaus of Alexandria (c. 70–140 CE): Life, Works & Roman Observations¶

Biographical & Astronomical Anchor (98 CE)¶

Menelaus of Alexandria (Μενέλαος ὁ Ἀλεξανδρεύς, as Pappus of Alexandria styles him in Synagoge VI) stands at the watershed between Hellenistic pure geometry and Imperial mathematical astronomy. Our firmest chronological anchor comes directly from Claudius Ptolemy’s Syntaxis Mathematica (Almagest VII.3), which records two precision stellar occultation observations made by Menelaus at Rome in the first year of Emperor Trajan (98 CE):

  1. On 15–16 Tybi (January 98 CE), Menelaus observed the star Spica (α Virginis) occulted by the Moon and emerging along the Moon’s western limb.

  2. A few nights later, on 18–19 Tybi, Menelaus measured the alignment of the northernmost star in the brow of Scorpius (β Scorpii) with the Moon’s southern horn.

Plutarch (De facie in orbe lunae, 930A) likewise introduces the mathematician “Menelaus” as an interlocutor in a philosophical-optical dialogue set at Rome, apologizing playfully to him before questioning the equality of angles of incidence and reflection.

Corpus of Mathematical Writings¶

Ancient Greek commentators and medieval Arabic bibliographers attest a wide mathematical output by Menelaus, of which only the Sphaerica survives intact:

  • *Sphaerica* (*Σφαιρικά* / *Sphaericorum Libri III*): Lost in its continuous Greek original save for fragments in Pappus and Theon of Alexandria, yet preserved completely in medieval Arabic, Hebrew, and Latin translations culminating in Edmond Halley’s critical Oxford edition (1758).

  • *On Chords in a Circle* (6 books): Attested by Theon of Alexandria (Commentary on Almagest I.10), who reports that Hipparchus wrote twelve books on chords in a circle and Menelaus six.

  • *Elements of Geometry* (3 books) & Direct Proof of Euclid I.25: Proclus (In Euclidem p. 345) preserves word-for-word Menelaus’s elegant direct proof of Euclid Elements I.25 (the converse Hinge Theorem), deliberately eliminating Euclid’s indirect reductio ad absurdum—the exact methodological signature visible across all three books of the Sphaerica.

  • Hydrostatics & Higher Curves: Arabic treatises preserve investigations by Menelaus into specific gravities of alloyed bodies and into paradoxical curves (including Viviani-type spherical intersections).
